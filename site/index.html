    From radio hiss to a photo

    How we caught it

    1. 1

      Predict the pass

      We take each satellite's orbital elements from CelesTrak, run SGP4 and work out to the second when Meteor-M N2-3 or N2-4 rises over a receiver. It's the same maths as the countdown above.

    2. 2

      Borrow an ear

      Radio hobbyists around the world share their receivers publicly as SpyServers. Right before the satellite rises, we connect to one, tune it to 137.9 MHz and stream the raw I/Q radio samples (about 190,000–310,000 per second) to our own computer for the whole 10–12 minute pass.

    3. 3

      Demodulate

      The satellite speaks LRPT: digital QPSK at 72,000 symbols per second. We run SatDump on the recording. It locks onto the carrier, tracks the Doppler shift as the satellite races past at 7 km/s and turns the waves into bits.

    4. 4

      Fix the errors

      Viterbi decoding and Reed–Solomon error correction clean up the noisy bits. Out come CCSDS frames carrying JPEG-compressed strips of the MSU-MR camera's scan lines, each about 1 km per pixel.

    5. 5

      Rebuild the picture

      Strips become channels: visible, near-infrared and thermal infrared. Layered together they make the colour composites you see, with the satellite's timestamps to say exactly when and where they were taken.

    Honest notes

    • Not our antenna. Every signal came through a volunteer's public receiver, credited on each photo. What we did ourselves: choose the pass, record the raw signal and decode it.
    • Why not the SatNOGS archive? We checked it first. The volunteer network's public "recordings" of Meteor passes are 48 kHz FM-demodulated audio, which can't carry a 72k-symbol digital signal. Its only images are ones the stations decoded themselves, and we never pass those off as ours.
    • Old US NOAA satellites? Their analogue APT signal is gone. A "good" NOAA-18 recording from 20 Oct 2026 has no 2400 Hz tone and no sync, so we went digital.
    • India first, when we can. There's no public receiver in India we can tune to 137.9 MHz yet, so each photo is labelled with the region it really shows.
    • Public receivers are imperfect. Most use broadband antennas, not satellite antennas, so many passes come in too weak to decode. Every attempt is logged below, failures included.
